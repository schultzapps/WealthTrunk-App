Where to keep the money

The calculator doesn't care which account you use — but the account you choose changes how the money is taxed, what it can be spent on, and who ends up controlling it. Here's how the common US options are treated.

US account types, described in general terms. Rules change and vary by state — confirm details before opening an account.
Account Tax treatment What it can be spent on Worth knowing
529 plan Grows tax-free for qualified education costs Tuition, fees, books, room and board; some K-12 and apprenticeships Many states offer a deduction. Limited Roth IRA rollovers are now allowed.
Coverdell ESA Grows tax-free for qualified education costs Broader K-12 coverage than a 529 Capped at $2,000 per year, with income limits on who can contribute.
UTMA / UGMA Some earnings taxed at the child's rate Anything that benefits the child Becomes the child's property at the age of majority. Counts heavily against financial aid.
Custodial brokerage Taxable Anything No tax break, but no restrictions and no strings either.
High-yield savings Interest taxed as income Anything No market risk, which matters when the money is needed in a few years rather than a decade.

These are US account types. WealthTrunk supports multiple currencies and works the same way wherever you are — the calculator above is currency-agnostic.

Why starting age matters so much

Drag the "child's age now" slider and watch the growth figure move. The contributions barely change — you're putting in a similar amount either way — but the growth changes dramatically, because each dollar has that many more years to compound.

  • Starting at birth gives an 18-year runway, long enough that growth often rivals contributions.
  • Starting at 10 roughly halves the runway, and growth becomes a much smaller share of the total.
  • A short horizon shifts the question from "how do I grow this?" to "how do I not lose it?"
  • Inflation works against you too. College costs and car prices climb over the same years you're saving, which is why the projection is shown in today's money rather than a larger future number.

These tools are illustrations, not advice. Projected returns are assumptions you choose, not predictions — real markets do not deliver a steady annual percentage. Nothing here is financial, tax, or investment advice.

Saving for kids: common questions

How much should I save for my child?

That depends entirely on what you're saving for and where. Rather than guess at a number, set a savings goal in the calculator and see what monthly amount reaches it by the age you choose. Working backwards from a target is more useful than any rule of thumb.

What's the difference between a 529, a Coverdell ESA, and a UTMA/UGMA?

A 529 plan grows tax-free when the money is spent on qualified education costs, and many states offer a deduction for contributing. A Coverdell ESA is similar but capped at $2,000 per year with income limits, though it covers K-12 costs more broadly. A UTMA/UGMA is a custodial account with no restriction on what the money is used for — but it legally becomes the child's property when they reach the age of majority in your state, and it weighs more heavily against financial aid. WealthTrunk tracks all three.

Does the money have to be used for college?

Depends on the account. A UTMA/UGMA or an ordinary brokerage account can be spent on anything — a car, a trade school, a house deposit, or handed over as a gift. A 529 or Coverdell is tax-advantaged specifically for education, though 529 rules have widened over time to include apprenticeships, some K-12 tuition, and limited Roth IRA rollovers.

What if my child is a newborn?

Set their age to 0. An 18-year horizon is where compounding does the most work — the difference between starting at birth and starting at age 10 is usually larger than most people expect, which you can see by dragging the age slider.

Should the money be invested or in savings?

That's a question about your time horizon and risk tolerance, not one this calculator can answer. A long horizon leaves room to ride out market swings; a short one usually doesn't. If you'd rather model a savings account, enter a rate close to what the account actually pays.

Is this financial advice?

No. It's arithmetic on the numbers you enter. Account rules, taxes, and financial aid treatment vary by state and change over time. Talk to a qualified professional.
